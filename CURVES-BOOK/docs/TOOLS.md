# The tools, and the rules of the practice

Every step of every construction in this workshop names the tool it is made with. The
names are the ones a draughtsman of 1947 would have used, and they are the ones the
practice board offers.

| Tool | What it may do | Classical? |
|---|---|---|
| **Straightedge** | The line through two points you already have. No marks, no measuring. | yes |
| **Compass** | A circle or an arc about a point you have, with a radius taken between two points you have. | yes |
| **Dividers** | Carry a length from one place to another; step off equal lengths along a line or a circle. (With Euclid's collapsing compass this takes a construction of its own; the modern compass keeps its opening, so the dividers only make the act explicit.) | yes, in effect |
| **Pencil** | Draw the curve through the points found, with a French curve or a steady hand. The curve itself is never constructed — only its points, tangents and normals are. | — |
| **Set square** | A perpendicular or a parallel through a point, at once. Classically a compass construction (Euclid I.11, I.12, I.31). | drafting |
| **Ruler** | Measure, or lay off a stated length. The trisection of an angle by the conchoid (Fig. 29) needs a ruler with two marks — a *neusis*, outside Euclid's rules; that is why the trisection is impossible with the first two tools and possible with this one. | drafting |
| **Protractor** | Lay off a stated angle. | drafting |
| **Rolling** | Not a tool but a motion: a circle rolling on a line or on a circle. The figure shows one position; the practice asks for that position and the point it carries. | mechanical |
| **Paper folding** | A crease that brings a point onto a line or a circle is a tangent of a conic. A crease is a line, so the practice board asks for the line. | — |
| **Linkage** | Bars and pivots. The book draws many (Peaucellier, Hart, the crossed parallelograms). | mechanical |

## Classical geometry

The rules of Euclid allow the straightedge and the compass, and nothing else. With them
one can bisect a segment or an angle, raise and drop perpendiculars, draw parallels,
copy an angle, and construct the points of any curve whose points are given by
*quadratic* relations in the data: circles, conics point by point, the cissoid, the
conchoid, the limacon, the lemniscate, the Cassinian ovals. One cannot construct, with
those two tools, a point whose position needs a *cubic* relation — the trisection of an
angle, the duplication of the cube — nor an *arc length*: that is why the cycloid, the
involute and the spiral of Archimedes are drawn point by point only approximately,
stepping off the chord for the arc, or exactly by a mechanism.

The book does not confine itself to Euclid: it uses the marked ruler, the carpenter's
square, rolling circles and linkages whenever they draw a curve well. The workshop
follows the book and says, at every step, which tool is in hand.

## The practice board

1. Open a figure, press **Practise this construction**.
2. The given elements are on the board. Read the instruction for the current step;
   the tool it names is selected for you (you may use any tool at any time — a
   construction line that the step did not ask for is kept, in grey).
3. Draw. The board snaps to the points you have, to the intersections of your lines and
   circles, and to the given curves. A drawn element turns green when it is what the
   step asks for; when the step is complete, the book's drawing of that step appears
   with its letters, and the next instruction follows.
4. **Hint** shows the next element as a dashed ghost for a few seconds. **Show** draws
   it for you (and counts). **Undo** takes back the last element.
5. At the end the board reports the number of elements, hints and shows, and the time;
   your best run is kept in the browser.

Keyboard: `1`–`6` choose the tools, `Esc` cancels a half-made element, `Ctrl+Z` undoes.

## On paper

Print the worksheet from the figure page (it prints the first step and the list of
instructions) and draw with real tools. A 2H pencil for the construction lines, an HB
for the curve; lay off lengths with dividers, not by reading a ruler; keep the compass
needle in the same hole for concentric arcs; join the points with a French curve, a
little at a time, overlapping the previous stroke.
